Book Project

My book project examines how international institutions with historically racist practices evolve toward more equal ones. I introduce the theory of "anti-racist capture," arguing that institutional transformation requires two conditions: sustained external pressure, through naming and shaming and the collective threat of member exit, and the presence of ideologically motivated bureaucratic entrepreneurs within the institution itself.

The project's primary case is the International Criminal Court, which spent its first two decades focusing almost exclusively on Africa before shifting to investigate individuals outside the continent. The book also examines the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade and the World Trade Organization's move to abandon its "colorblind" approach, tracing gains in the bargaining power of developing countries and the evolution of the WTO's Dispute Settlement Mechanism toward greater substantive equality. To test the theory's necessity claims, I process-trace two negative cases, the League of Nations Mandates System and UN Peacekeeping Operations, in which sustained naming and shaming failed to produce change in the absence of viable exit threats or influential internal advocates.

The project contributes to theories of institutional transformation and to the study of race in international relations, identifying the conditions under which progress against global racial hierarchy is achieved.

"The Efficacy of a ‘Digital Witness’ for Transnational Advocacy Organizations." Preparing for submission.
[What is the influence of digital investigative technologies on transnational advocacy organizations' (TAOs) network power? This paper argues that the ability to create a "digital witness" using tools such as GIS, satellite imagery, open-source intelligence, and 3D spatial reconstruction reduces constraints on TAOs by broadening two forms of network power: access and brokerage. TAOs using digital investigations gain greater access power by becoming less constrained by the lack of vested enforcement authority, and greater brokerage power by connecting otherwise inaccessible rights violators — such as Russia and Sudan's Rapid Support Forces — to international courts and law. The paper also demonstrates how TAOs have become first movers in creating international soft law on digital investigations, as evidenced by the Berkeley Protocol on Digital Open-Source Investigations. Findings draw on semi-structured interviews with human rights advocates and lawyers and case studies of the wars in Ukraine and Sudan.]

"“Dropped in the Middle of Nowhere” How Third-Country Deportations Offshore Legal Violence, Racialize Migration, and Reproduce Tiered Sovereignty." (with Thomas J. Rachko, Jr.) Preparing for submission.
[This paper examines how third-country deportations (the practice of deporting migrants to countries where they lack citizenship or legal ties) offshore legal violence and racialize migration diplomacy. IR scholarship on migration diplomacy is overwhelmingly built on great power politics, distinguishing between cooperative and coercive modes of migration policy. We complicate this literature by asking whether the racial identity of the receiving state itself functions as a form of currency: does a state's position in the global racial hierarchy affect what it can extract in a deal, or how much scrutiny the arrangement draws? We argue that third-country deportation agreements are a contemporary expression of colonial racial hierarchy, in which receiving states occupy the position of the "semi-civilized"/not fully sovereign equals, but useful intermediaries for powerful states. The paper draws on an original dataset of global third-country deportation dyads, disaggregated by agreement type]
